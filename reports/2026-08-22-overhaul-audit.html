Assumptions

Traffic is low. I have no GA4 access from here. Given the site went live this spring, has a 64/100 mobile PageSpeed score, thin page count, and a canonical bug actively pointing /tools at a dead route, I am assuming organic search traffic is negligible and total traffic is tens to low hundreds of sessions a month, mostly LinkedIn and direct. 01Current state diagnosis

The feedback you had is right, and the evidence is specific. Here is what the old brief actually did to the site.

The positioning contradicts itself across surfaces

The homepage meta title says the site is for “Marketing & Comms Professionals”. The on-page intro says it is for “people who want to use AI well, without having to become an expert first”, which is everyone, which is no one. The project context file (CLAUDE.md) says the audience is “smart women in marketing and communications”. Three surfaces, three audiences, none of them your buyer. And the fourth surface is the one that will actively fight you: CLAUDE.md contains a standing rule that reads “Never put charity-sector framing on the site”. That rule enforced the old two-track firewall, the firewall is lifted, and the rule is still live. Any build session you run in October will obey it unless it is rewritten first. It goes early in the action list for that reason.

The taxonomy describes tools, not jobs

Six categories: Writing, Research, Design, Video, Automation, Building. These classify what a tool is, which is how every general directory organises, and exactly what a comms lead does not think in. Nobody opens a directory holding the problem “I need a Writing tool”. They hold “the spring appeal needs to go out” or “we have to caption everything now”. Worse, the biggest category is Building, with 21 of 66 rows: Cursor, Windsurf, Postman, Replit, Gemini CLI, Dify, OpenClaw, and fourteen more. Nearly a third of the directory is developer tooling that documents your own build journey. That story belongs on My Stack, where it already lives and works as proof of practice. In the buyer-facing directory it is ballast.

The rows cannot answer the buyer's questions

Each row carries name, category, status, cost, verdict, url. Not one field answers what a charity comms lead actually needs to know: where the data sits, whether the tool trains on input, whether a nonprofit tier exists, whether adoption likely triggers a DPIA, whether it can be explained to a trustee. The gap is visible even inside rows that are otherwise fine: the Canva row prices it at “Free tier / Pro from £13/mo” while Canva Pro is free for registered charities. The single most valuable fact about that tool for your audience is absent from its row.

The verdicts are capability judgements in a general voice

The verdicts are well written and honest, which is worth saying because it means the voice survives the re-point intact. But they judge on capability: “The workhorse”, “The category leader for no-code automation”, “My primary tool”. A general reader learns whether a tool is good. Your buyer needs to know whether it is safe, fundable and defensible, and no verdict currently touches that. This is why pausing the 68 rewrites was the right call: every one written before the new axis exists gets written twice.

The status axis is about you, not the reader

in_stack versus on_radar records your usage. Under the new premise, the reader's question is “has this passed the checks”, not “does Jasmin run it”. And the current implementation half-works anyway: only in_stack renders a badge, so the 47 on_radar rows, 71% of the directory, display no signal at all.

The conversion layer is a form into a void

The only capture on the site is “Get The Edit in your inbox”, a footer form writing to a Supabase table that nothing reads and nothing sends from. There is no welcome email, no asset exchanged, no delivery mechanism of any kind. A visitor who subscribes today hears nothing, ever. The Subscribe page promises a “fortnightly digest” that does not exist. Separately, the Submit-a-tool form silently discards every submission (a known finding from the July security audit). The one working piece of conversion infrastructure is the “Work with me” link in the nav and footer, which is real and stays.

Site debt that matters for this overhaul, and the debt that does not

From the August gates audit, exactly three findings earn a place in this plan, because the re-point is partly an SEO play (“AI tools for charities” is a winnable query in a way “AI tools” never was): /tools canonicalises to a dead /toolkit route, so the most important page on the site tells Google to index a 404; /submit and /stack ship no meta; and every non-home page reuses the homepage OG card. The rest of the gates list (contrast failures, heading skips, matter-js bundle weight) stays parked. It fails the two-week rule and none of it blocks the re-point.

02Re-pointed positioning

The premise, as it should read on the About panel, and the source for every meta description, OG title and homepage intro rewrite in section 6:

The Edit is an opinionated directory of AI tools for communications teams in charities, cultural organisations and heritage. Every tool here is judged on the questions this sector actually has to answer before adopting anything: where your data sits, whether the tool trains on what you type into it, whether there is a nonprofit price, whether using it is likely to need a DPIA, and whether you could explain it to a trustee in one sentence. No sponsored listings, no affiliate links, and no tool appears until it has been through the checks. Built and maintained by Jasmin Aziz, a strategic communications consultant who works with exactly these teams.

Two deliberate choices in that paragraph. “No tool appears until it has been through the checks” converts the shrinking row count from a weakness into the product. And the last sentence does the consultancy hand-off without a pitch: the reader who wants more than a directory has already been told who built it and why she knows.

03New evaluation axis and taxonomy

The five sector fields

These become columns on every row. Definitions matter more than names, because loose definitions are how this collapses back into a general directory. Precision here is also credibility: this audience contains people who run DPIAs, and sloppy language about them reads as marketing.

Trustee noteOne sentenceThe sentence you could say at a board meeting with no follow-up questions you can't answer. If it can't be written, that is the verdict.

The verdict column stays, rewritten against this axis: the sector lens becomes the argument of the verdict, and the five fields are its evidence. The in_stack/on_radar status column is retired and replaced by what actually matters now, a Recommended / Judged, not recommended distinction covered in section 4.

The comms-job taxonomy

Six jobs replace the six tool-type categories: Appeals & fundraising · Case studies & storytelling · Social · Internal comms · Accessibility · Translation. One structural change from the current build: a tool can hold more than one job. Canva is Social and Appeals; Descript is Accessibility and Case studies. So jobs is a multi-value field (comma-separated in the Sheet) and the filter matches on contains rather than equals. That is a small, contained change in the code and it is what makes six jobs enough; without multi-tagging you would need a “General” category within a fortnight, and that is the first step back toward the directory you are leaving.

04Content migration plan

Judged row by row against the new axis, the live 66 splits four ways.

Survive and re-verdict: roughly 30 rows

The core assistants (Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot, Google Workspace AI, Notion AI), the research set (Perplexity, NotebookLM, Granola, Notion), the design set a comms team would actually run (Canva, Adobe Suite, Firefly, Ideogram, Photoroom), the accessible-content set (Descript, ElevenLabs, Opus Clip, Submagic, HeyGen), presentation and drafting (Gamma, Wispr Flow), and the workflow layer (Zapier, Airtable, HubSpot). Every one keeps its name and url and gets new sector fields plus a rewritten verdict. Nothing survives as-is: a row without the five fields is by definition not finished, so the honest answer to “which survive untouched” is none.

Cut: roughly 33 rows

All of Building except nothing: the full 21 (Lovable, Vercel, GitHub, Supabase, Claude Code, Cursor, Windsurf, Postman, Replit, Gemini CLI, Dify, OpenClaw, 21st.dev, Google Stitch, Manus, Glaze, Framer, Netlify, Floot, Zo Computer, Google AI Studio) leave the directory. The ones you personally run already live on My Stack, which stays exactly as it is; the build-journey story is portfolio proof and loses nothing by moving out of the buyer's way. 05Capture mechanism

The structural decision first: do not build email infrastructure. The Supabase table is a dead end (nothing reads it, nothing sends), and standing up an email platform to gate one file is exactly the over-building your standing rule exists to catch. You already own the delivery mechanism: Substack. Gate the template as a subscriber-only Substack post. Substack handles the capture, the confirmation email, the delivery and the list, all built, all free, all yours. The funnel you drew (directory attracts → template captures → Substack builds trust → site converts) keeps every stage; capture and trust simply happen on the same platform, which for a solo operator is a simplification with no cost. The Supabase table stays as a historical record and takes no further writes.

The two automations become one system

The site has two automated writers, and neither survives the re-point unchanged, because both were architected for the old brief. The principle for their rebuild is the one already locked on the Transition Board for the delivery build: machines maintain facts, you own judgement. Split the seven fields accordingly: pricing, nonprofit tier, data location and training policy are machine-verifiable against published sources; the DPIA flag, trustee note and verdict are judgement, and no automation ever writes them.

The fortnightly Cowork task becomes the checks engine. Today it writes tool rows against the old brief (verified in the account registry by absence: it is a desktop task, and the 61-to-66 row growth since July in the old voice looks like its work; confirm its prompt in the Cowork app). Rebuilt, its job is: re-fetch the sources behind the fact fields for every live row, update them with a source link, stamp last_checked, and where a factual change touches a judgement field (a training policy changes, so a DPIA flag may need moving) flag it to you and write nothing. Before October, trim it to flag-only or pause it so it stops writing old-brief rows.

The whats_new daily Routine gets re-pointed, not left alone. Piping five general AI stories a day at a sector audience is the last prosumer surface on the site, and it is derivative content with no moat. One prompt edit changes the extraction rule from “up to 5 stories” to “only stories touching tools in the directory or the sector: nonprofit programmes, data and training policy changes, regulation, accessibility”. Zero-story days become correct behaviour. The checks engine feeds the same surface for free: “Canva changed its AI training terms, checked 3 Nov” is currency no general directory can publish, and What’s New becomes the changelog of the checks rather than a newsletter summary.

The pipeline itself can likely shrink from four hops to one. The Routine’s GitHub Actions relay exists solely because the 2026 Routine sandbox could not reach script.google.com. Tested from the Cowork cloud environment on 22 Aug: script.google.com is reachable (the request connected and drew an application response; the redirect-following behaviour still needs a two-minute verification before committing). If that verifies, both writers become task → Apps Script → Sheet, the shared secret finally goes into the Apps Script, and the GitHub relay and its watchdog get deleted. If it does not verify, keep the relay: it works and is monitored, and a working ugly pipe beats an elegant broken one mid-relaunch. Either way the pipe work is not week-critical, because it changes nothing a visitor sees; it can follow the relaunch.

Left alone, on purposeMy Stack (proof of practice, already works), Design Kit (general-audience but zero-cost to leave; revisit only if a client conversation touches it), nav structure, the Submit page decision (pull it or wire it, 20 minutes, but it fails the two-week rule and can wait), and all remaining gates-audit debt.

The recommendation

First task, Monday morning of the admin week: lock the evaluation axis. Not the code, not the copy, not the Sheet. Ninety minutes with section 3 of this document, amend what you disagree with, freeze the five fields and their definitions, and only then touch anything else.

It goes first because it is the only task every other task depends on: the triage needs it to judge rows, the research pass needs it to know what to verify, the verdicts need it to have an argument, the card design needs it to know what to render, and the capture copy needs it to name what the checks are. It is also the moat itself; the rows, the filters and the template are all just the axis made visible. You have already paid once for building content before the brief existed, when 68 verdicts started getting rewritten against a positioning you were about to abandon. Locking the axis first is the same lesson, applied in advance this time.
