## How to use this

This is a working template, not a finished policy. It assumes you have no
legal team, no IT department, and no appetite for a document nobody reads.

Fill in the square brackets. Delete what does not apply to you. Take it to
your senior team, then your board. The point is to end up with something
short enough that your staff will actually read it and specific enough that
it changes what they do on Monday.

A policy PDF in a shared drive is not governance. Governance is a named
person, a gate, and a data rule. This template gives you all three.

Expect it to take two meetings and an afternoon.

## 1. Why we have this policy

[ORGANISATION] uses AI tools in its work. Staff are already using them,
whether or not we have written anything down. This policy exists so that use
is deliberate rather than accidental, and so that the people responsible for
this organisation can answer three questions with confidence:

- What AI tools are we using, and for what?
- What happens to the information we put into them?
- Who is accountable when something goes wrong?

This policy applies to everyone who works for or on behalf of [ORGANISATION],
including staff, trustees, volunteers, freelancers and contractors.

---

## 2. What we mean by AI

This policy covers **generative AI tools**: systems that produce text, images,
audio, video or code in response to a prompt. Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini and
Copilot are the common ones. It also covers tools with AI built into them,
such as meeting note-takers, transcription services and design tools with
generative features.

Two terms are worth knowing, because the risk is different for each.

**Conversational AI** responds to what you type. It produces text or images.
It takes no action of its own. You decide what to do with the output.

**Agentic AI** pursues a goal across several steps, using tools you have
connected to it. It can send, book, file or publish. The output is an action,
not a draft.

A **connector** is a permission you give an AI tool to reach another system
you use: your inbox, your calendar, your files, your CRM. A connector
inherits the access of the account you sign in with. If you connect it to
your full work email, it can see everything you can see.

---

## 3. The five things this policy sets up

1. **An audit.** We know what is actually in use.
2. **A risk classification.** We know which uses are low risk and which are not.
3. **A named owner.** One person is accountable.
4. **A gate.** New tools get checked before they are adopted.
5. **Training.** People know what not to do, not just what to do.

The gate matters more than its complexity. A one-page check that actually
happens beats a procurement process that gets skipped.

---

## 4. Who is responsible

**[NAME / ROLE] is the AI lead for [ORGANISATION].** They are responsible for
maintaining the tool register, running the gate on new tools, and reporting
to [SENIOR TEAM / BOARD] on AI use [ANNUALLY / TWICE A YEAR].

This is not a technical role. It does not require the postholder to
understand how the models work. It requires them to keep a list, ask the
questions in section 8, and escalate what they cannot answer.

**Every member of staff** is responsible for the output they send. AI drafts.
A person approves. That responsibility does not transfer to the tool.

**The [BOARD / TRUSTEES]** are responsible for satisfying themselves that this
policy exists, is followed, and is reviewed. They are not responsible for
approving individual tools.

---

## 5. What staff may and may not do

Five rules. They apply to everyone, every time.

**One. Treat any AI tool as a system you do not control.** Anything you put
in is in. Anything it puts out is your responsibility.

**Two. Personal data does not belong in a consumer AI tool.** That means
names, contact details, financial information, and anything about specific
supporters, beneficiaries, service users, children, or colleagues. UK GDPR
applies whether or not you remember it does. If you are unsure whether
something counts as personal data, assume it does and ask [NAME / ROLE].

**Three. Check before you send.** Three questions, thirty seconds, every time
you are about to act on AI output for anything that matters:

- Is there anything here I cannot personally verify?
- Does it contain a specific claim, a figure, a date, a name, an attribution,
  that I should check against the source?
- Would I put my name to this without a second read?

**Four. A person approves anything that leaves the building.** Drafts are AI
work. Sent communications are human work, even when the AI wrote most of the
words. This applies to anything reaching a supporter, a funder, a beneficiary,
a journalist or the public.

**Five. Some tasks are not for AI.** See section 7.

## 6. What we use, and what we put into it

[ORGANISATION] maintains a register of approved AI tools. It lists, for each
tool: what it is used for, who owns it, what plan we are on, where the data
is held, and whether the provider trains on what we put in.

The register lives at [LOCATION] and is maintained by [NAME / ROLE].

**Approved tools and approved uses**

| Tool | What it is for | Plan | May it receive personal data? |
|---|---|---|---|
| [TOOL] | [USE] | [PLAN] | [YES, under [CONDITIONS] / NO] |
| [TOOL] | [USE] | [PLAN] | [YES, under [CONDITIONS] / NO] |

**Where the data sits matters, and so does the plan.** The same tool behaves
differently on different tiers. Four positions, in order of how much you can
rely on them:

1. **Consumer plan with training switched off in settings.** Acceptable for
   low-sensitivity daily work: drafting from public material, rewording,
   summarising documents that contain no personal data. This relies on the
   toggle doing what it says and the provider's privacy policy matching it.
   That is not a safe assumption for anything involving identifiable people.
2. **Paid individual plan.** Better terms, usually. Read them. Do not assume.
3. **Team or enterprise plan** with a data processing agreement in place.
   Appropriate for organisational drafting and internal workflows. Terms vary
   significantly between providers, so check the one you are signing.
4. **No AI tool at all.** The correct answer for some categories. See
   section 7.

If you do not know which position a tool sits in, it is not approved yet.

## 7. Where AI does not belong

Some tasks are excluded not because the output would be worse, but because a
person doing the work is the point.

At [ORGANISATION] that includes:

- [Pastoral and welfare conversations]
- [Safeguarding assessments, records and decisions]
- [Condolence, bereavement and crisis correspondence]
- [Individual supporter or beneficiary correspondence where the relationship
  is the service]
- [Anything a named individual would reasonably expect a person wrote]
- [ADD YOUR OWN]

Discuss this list with your team and make it real. The generic version is
worth nothing. The version your staff argued about is worth a lot.

A supporter receiving a personalised letter that an AI generated and nobody
read is receiving something different from a letter a person wrote, even when
the words are identical.

## 8. The gate: adopting a new tool

Before [ORGANISATION] adopts any new AI tool, [NAME / ROLE] answers these
questions on one page. If a question cannot be answered, the tool is not
adopted.

1. **What is it for?** Name the job, not the category.
2. **Who will use it, and what will they put into it?**
3. **Where is the data held?** UK, EU, US, or unclear.
4. **Does the provider train on what we put in?** On our plan specifically,
   not on their enterprise tier.
5. **Is there a nonprofit or charity tier?** Many providers have one and do
   not advertise it.
6. **What can it do without a person approving?** If the answer is anything
   at all, see section 9.
7. **Is this likely to need a DPIA?** See section 10.
8. **What happens if we stop using it?** Can we get our content out.

A tool that clears the gate goes into the register in section 6. A tool that
does not is either declined or escalated to [SENIOR TEAM].

Nobody signs up to a new AI tool with an [ORGANISATION] email address without
this check.

---

## 9. Agentic tools and connectors

Where a tool can take action rather than produce a draft, three additional
questions apply before it is approved:

1. **What actions can it take?**
2. **Who reviews before it acts?**
3. **What is the escalation path when it does something wrong?**

Two things change once a tool can act. Actions can be irreversible: you can
ignore a bad draft, you cannot unsend a bad email. And failures can be
silent: in a conversation you see every response, in an automated workflow the
intermediate steps may never be seen by anyone.

Connectors are granted by [NAME / ROLE] and reviewed [ANNUALLY]. A connector
is disconnected when the person who set it up changes role or leaves, or when
the tool stops being used.

---

## 10. Data protection and DPIAs

A Data Protection Impact Assessment is a legal requirement under UK GDPR for
processing likely to result in a high risk to people's rights. AI processing
of personal data will often meet that bar. This is an ICO requirement, not a
recommendation.

To be precise about what the requirement is: organisations carry out DPIAs.
Tools do not have them and cannot provide them. The question is never whether
a tool "has" a DPIA. It is whether your intended use of it triggers one.

**[ORGANISATION] will complete a DPIA before** [any AI processing of
supporter, beneficiary or service-user personal data / any use of an AI tool
that takes action without human review / ADD YOUR OWN TRIGGERS].

Our data protection lead is [NAME / ROLE]. Where this policy and our data
protection policy conflict, the data protection policy takes precedence.

[ORGANISATION] is registered with the ICO under registration [NUMBER].

## 11. What we tell people

Openness is cheaper than being found out.

**Staff.** This policy is shared with everyone on joining and whenever it is
updated. Nobody is disciplined for asking whether something is allowed. People
are accountable for using a tool they knew was not approved.

**Trustees.** [NAME / ROLE] reports on AI use [ANNUALLY / TWICE A YEAR],
covering: what is in the register, what changed, anything that went wrong, and
anything the board needs to decide.

**Supporters, funders and the public.** [ORGANISATION] [does / does not]
disclose AI involvement in its communications. Where we do:

- We do not present AI-generated content as the personal writing of a named
  individual without that person having written or substantially edited it.
- We do not use AI-generated images of people in a way that implies they are
  real beneficiaries, supporters or staff.
- [ADD YOUR POSITION ON DISCLOSURE IN FUNDING APPLICATIONS]

Funders increasingly ask. Have the answer before the question.

---

## 12. When something goes wrong

If AI output has caused or may cause harm, including an inaccuracy that
reached someone outside the organisation, a personal data breach, or an
automated action nobody intended:

1. Tell [NAME / ROLE] the same day. Do not wait to establish whether it is
   serious.
2. [NAME / ROLE] assesses whether it is a personal data breach. If it is,
   our data protection policy governs what happens next, including the
   72-hour reporting window.
3. [NAME / ROLE] records what happened and what changed as a result.

Reporting early is treated as good practice, not as an admission.

## Appendix B: The check before you send

Print it. Stick it near the people who send things.

Before you act on any AI output that matters:

1. Is there anything here I cannot personally verify?
2. Does it contain a figure, date, name or attribution I should check against
   the source?
3. Would I put my name to this without a second read?

Three questions. Thirty seconds. It is the difference between the
organisations that get caught out by AI and the ones that do not.
