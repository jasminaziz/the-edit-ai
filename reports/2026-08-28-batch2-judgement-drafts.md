### Blotato · Amber · Social

**Verdict draft:**

> The pitch is one story becoming a week of posts across every channel, and
> for a one-person team that's a real temptation. Treat it as a drafting
> engine rather than an autopilot: supporters follow charities for the
> human voice, and bulk-generated posts read like syndication fast. Worth
> knowing before you connect your accounts: the vendor publishes no
> position on whether it trains on your content, and no charity price. At
> $29 a month you're paying for volume, so make sure volume is actually
> your problem.

**Trustee note:** We use it to draft social posts faster; nothing goes out
without a person editing it, and no supporter data goes in.

**Reasoning.** The catch is editorial (syndicated voice), which is the use
problem, with the Unclear training position as the one governance clause it
earns because connecting social accounts is the risk moment. Amber: general
drafting is fine; supporter content and account access put personal data in.

### Ideogram · Amber · Social, Case studies & storytelling

**Verdict draft:**

> The thing it does better than the big names is words inside images:
> posters, event graphics, quote cards that don't come out with mangled
> letters. That's a genuine comms job. But it trains on everything you give
> it with no way to opt out, says nothing about where your data lives, and
> the cheapest paid tier is now $20 a month with no charity price. Adobe
> Express does most of the same jobs, on licensed training data, free for
> charities on 50 seats. Reach for this one only when the type really
> matters.

**Trustee note:** We use it for graphics with text in them; photos of real
people don't go in, because it learns from whatever it's given.

**Reasoning.** Leads with the one genuine differentiator, then the buying
advice a general directory wouldn't give: the free charity alternative
covers most of the ground. Training-with-no-opt-out earns its sentence
because it's the reason for the photo rule. Amber: prompt-driven graphics
are low-risk until identifiable people go in.

### Granola · RED · Research, Internal comms

**Verdict draft:**

> My meeting notes tool, and the reason is that it doesn't send a bot into
> the call: it runs on your machine and writes notes from what it hears,
> which makes it the least intrusive of its kind to actually use. It's also
> the clearest case on this list for asking permission first. It hears
> everyone in the room, transcripts stay on your account indefinitely, and
> the tiers a small team would buy have training on by default with an
> opt-out to find. Turn that off, tell people you're using it, and it earns
> its keep in every internal meeting. The DPA is published, which most
> tools this size don't bother with.

**Trustee note:** We tell everyone in a meeting when it's running, training
is switched off, and recordings aren't kept, only notes.

**Reasoning.** First person is earned: it's in Jasmin's stack. **The Red is
the contentious call, so the reasoning is shown in full.** Amendment 4 says
the flag describes the default configuration, and Amber's definition is
"fine for general work, DPIA territory once supporter, beneficiary or staff
data goes in." Granola has no general work: its only function is
processing colleagues' voices, so staff personal data goes in the moment it
is used at all, with indefinite transcript retention and training on by
default. The boundary rule says a boundary case takes the cautious side.
The verdict and trustee note then carry the better-configured story, which
is exactly the Copilot pattern from amendment 4. If Jasmin rules Amber
instead, the Wispr Flow precedent (Amber, cloud transcription, audio
stored) is the argument for it.

### Submagic · Amber · Accessibility, Social

**Verdict draft:**

> Captions on your social video in minutes, which matters because most
> supporters watch with the sound off and captioning is the accessibility
> job small teams skip. It's quicker and cheaper than Descript for that one
> job; Descript wins the moment you want to actually edit the footage. Two
> things to weigh: the free tier watermarks everything, and the vendor's
> privacy notice says nothing about whether uploads train its models, which
> is a question to ask before beneficiary faces go through it. Starter is
> $19 a month, or $12 if you pay the year.

**Trustee note:** We use it to caption videos already approved for
publishing, so nothing goes in that isn't already public.

**Reasoning.** Positioned against Descript, which is the honest comparison
the reader needs. Amber on the Descript precedent; the Unclear training
position is carried in the verdict because faces in video are the sector's
real exposure. The annual-billing trap is stated per the currency ruling.

### Gamma · Amber · Internal comms, Case studies & storytelling

**Verdict draft:**

> An outline becomes a presentable deck in minutes, and for internal
> updates, project briefings and anything where the deadline beats the
> design, that's real time back. Two catches. Everything it makes has a
> family resemblance, so give it your colours and fonts before the whole
> organisation's decks converge on the same look. And on the individual
> plans a small team would buy, it trains on your content unless you switch
> that off in settings, so do that on day one. No charity price, and the
> pricing page keeps its numbers to itself, so check what you'd pay before

**Trustee note:** We use it for internal presentations, training is turned
off, and nothing about a named person goes into it.

**Reasoning.** The main catch is aesthetic convergence, which is a use
problem and true everywhere; the training default earns one sentence
because the fix is a setting. The unverifiable pricing is stated plainly
because the site's premise is checked facts.

### Gemini, consumer app · Amber · Research, Internal comms

**Verdict draft:**

> This row is the consumer Gemini app, and the main thing to know is that
> most charities don't need it. On the free Google for Nonprofits edition
> you get the same Gemini app on Workspace terms, where it doesn't train on
> your chats. The consumer version does train by default, a sample of
> conversations is read by humans, and reviewed chats are kept for up to
> three years even if you delete them. Capable model, wrong door: if your
> organisation is on Google for Nonprofits, walk through the Workspace one.

**Trustee note:** We use Gemini through our Google for Nonprofits account,
not the consumer app, so our conversations aren't used for training.

**Reasoning.** The entire value of this row is routing the reader to the
free, safe door they already own, which is buying advice no general
directory gives. Governance carries the middle because it's the reason for
the routing, the Copilot exception applied. Amber on the ChatGPT precedent:
same training value, same default posture.

### Seedance · RED · Case studies & storytelling, Social — FAILURE ROW

**Verdict draft:**

> Here because people are asking about it, not because you should adopt it.
> The video generation is genuinely impressive and the free credits are
> tempting for a team that could never afford a film crew. The hosted
> product is ByteDance's: your data sits on servers in the US, Singapore
> and Malaysia under a Singapore-law policy, it trains on what you give it,
> and there's no opt-out and no UK footing to speak of. For a charity,
> anything with a real person in it is out of the question, and a tool you
> can only use for things that don't matter isn't a tool. If generated
> video earns a place in your work, wait for a vendor you could explain to
> your board.

**Trustee note:** We don't use it; its data terms can't be squared with our
obligations to the people in our films.

**Reasoning.** DeepSeek pattern: the honest capability case stays so the
judgement doesn't read as dismissal, then governance leads because it's why
the row fails. "A tool you can only use for things that don't matter isn't
a tool" is the argument in one sentence.

### Grok · RED · Research — FAILURE ROW

**Verdict draft:**

> The honest capability case first: the model is quick, current and free to
> try at grok.com these days, no X subscription required. The reason it
> fails the checks is what surrounds it. It trains on your conversations
> unless you find the opt-out, and there's a second, separate opt-out
> buried in X's settings for what X shares with it; even switched off, Grok
> still learns from your interactions with the X features it powers. That's
> a data relationship you can't fully leave while your organisation is on
> X. As a research toy on public information, harmless. For anything
> touching your supporters, your drafts or your judgement calls, use a tool
> whose off switch is an off switch.

**Trustee note:** We don't put organisational content into it; its data
settings can't be fully switched off while we're on X.

**Reasoning.** Opens by correcting the stale access claim (28 Aug pricing
check), because publishing a failure row with a wrong fact is the worst
combination. **Red, with the counterargument recorded:** ChatGPT carries
the same trains-unless-you-opt-out value at Amber. The distinguishing,
sourced fact is that Grok's opt-out doesn't fully opt out: X-feature
interactions keep teaching it regardless, which fails the "off switch"
test no other Amber row fails. If Jasmin weighs the precedent more heavily
than the entanglement, Amber is defensible; the verdict text works either
way.
