## What the first pass got wrong

The first fifteen drafts turned every verdict into a governance note. Jasmin
stopped them: the verdicts have to be centred on the use of the tools, and this
is not a governance-only website.

The structural proof is on the card. A ToolCard already renders **Where your
data sits**, **Trains on your content**, **Nonprofit pricing**, the **DPIA
chip** and **Say this to a trustee**. Five governance elements. A verdict that
is also governance makes the card say one thing six times and never say whether
the tool is any good.

The audit agrees, and it is canonical on strategy:

> Each row carries name, category, status, cost, verdict, url. Not one field
> answers what a charity comms lead actually needs to know: where the data sits,
> whether the tool trains on input, whether a nonprofit tier exists, whether
> adoption likely triggers a DPIA, whether it can be explained to a trustee.

The seven fields were **added** because the verdict could not answer adoption
questions. The audit never asked for the verdict to become one, and it describes
rows like Canva's as otherwise fine. **A5 is an audience change, not a subject
change.** The old verdicts speak to a general reader. The new ones speak to a
charity, cultural or heritage comms lead. They are still about using the tools.

The model was already in the Sheet. Nineteen `my_stack` verdicts in Jasmin's own
voice, and only one is about data governance: Microsoft Copilot, where
governance is the reason to use the tool. The first pass took Copilot's shape
and applied it to all fifteen.

## The settled spec

**Identity, ruled by Jasmin 26 August 2026: The Edit is a comms resource first,
with a compliance edge.**

| Slot | Job |
|---|---|
| **The axis** | Filters and gates. Decides whether a row appears at all, and answers whether a tool can be adopted safely. |
| **The verdict** | Recommends. What this is for, whether it is worth your time, where it lets you down. |
| **The trustee note** | The sentence for the board. |

Rules that follow, all ruled 26 August:

1. **Centre of gravity is the work.** Governance enters a verdict only where a
   governance fact is the reason to use or avoid that specific tool. Everywhere
   else the fields already carry it.
2. **Name the catch, read widely.** The catch is whatever disappoints: a free
   tier that is useless, a setup cost, a learning curve, output that needs more
   editing than writing fresh, an editorial line you should not cross.
   Sometimes it is the data problem. Usually it is not.
3. **Voice.** First person where it is evidence Jasmin has earned. Second person
   for the recommendation. Four of the fifteen carry first person; the rest
   would be borrowing authority rather than showing it.
4. **Sector translation.** The **job** is sector-specific: a funder report, an
   appeal, a board pack, an exhibition text, a supporter's testimony. The tool
   assessment is not re-derived for charities, because Descript removing the
   barrier to captioning is true everywhere.
5. **Voice rules unchanged.** UK English, contractions, no em dashes, no inline
   quote marks, direct, no balanced surveys.

## The fifteen

### HubSpot · Sheet row 3 · Red · Appeals & fundraising, Internal comms

> The free tier is genuinely usable, which is why so many small charities land
> here rather than on a fundraising-specific CRM. It'll hold your supporter
> list, your appeal emails and your corporate pipeline in one place. It thinks
> in deals rather than donations, so you're translating your own language into
> its fields, and that gets harder as you grow. Slow down on the AI features
> specifically: they sit straight on top of your supporter records, and the free
> tier is the one with the least control over where those go.

**Reasoning.** The real catch is not the DPIA flag, it is that a sales CRM is
doing a fundraising job and you pay for that in translation forever. Governance
gets the last sentence because on this row it is the reason for the Red: the AI
features sit on the supporter database, and A4 established that the free tier is
assigned to the US with training on by default.

### Canva · Sheet row 27 · Amber · Social, Case studies & storytelling

> If you have no designer, this is what gets you from nothing to a usable social
> set in an afternoon. The highest-value thing you can do with it isn't the AI:
> pay a designer once to build reusable branded templates, then your team makes
> its own campaign artwork instead of commissioning every time. Free Pro for
> charities on 50 users makes that a cheap decision. Skip that step and
> everything you make looks like Canva. The AI is far better at resizing and
> adapting what you already have than at originating anything.

**Reasoning.** Built from Jasmin's preserved column L text, which was already
the sector answer: the value is team capacity, not the AI features. Two catches,
both about the work. Everything looks like Canva without the brand setup, and
the AI adapts rather than originates. The training ambiguity stays in the field
and the trustee note, where it already is.

### Adobe Creative Cloud · Sheet row 28 · Amber · Case studies & storytelling

> Worth it if someone in the building already knows these tools, and hard to
> justify if nobody does. For exhibition graphics, print, or anything where
> typography has to be right, there's still no substitute, and the generative
> features inside Photoshop and Illustrator sit in the workflow rather than
> sending you somewhere else. Charity Digital Exchange is the route to the
> discount and it's a meaningful one. If your output is mostly social and mostly
> fast, Canva will serve you better and cost you nothing.

**Reasoning.** The catch here is skills, not data. A discounted licence is
worthless to a team with nobody who can drive it, and that is the honest advice
for most organisations this size. Exhibition graphics and print name the
cultural and heritage half of the audience specifically.

### Adobe Express · Sheet row 29 · Amber · Social, Case studies & storytelling

> The AI image generator to reach for when the image is going in an appeal or a
> funder report, because Firefly is trained on Adobe Stock and licensed content
> rather than whatever was scraped. That matters more in this sector than most:
> you're publishing to people who will ask where things came from. Free Premium
> for charities on 50 seats. It isn't the most inventive generator on the
> market, and if your team already lives in Canva, a second design tool costs
> more in confusion than it saves in output.

**Reasoning.** Provenance is a use argument here rather than a compliance one:
the reason it matters is that funders and supporters ask. The second catch is
tool sprawl, which is the practical risk for a two-person team, and it is the
kind of thing a directory should say out loud.

### Perplexity · Sheet row 39 · Amber · Research

> Where I start when I need an evidence base rather than an answer: sector
> statistics for an appeal, background for a bid, what a policy change means for
> your organisation in practice. Deep Research is the part that earns the money,
> coming back with a cited report rather than a page of links. My workflow is
> Perplexity for the evidence, Claude to turn it into the plan. The citations
> make it feel finished, which is the trap. Open the sources before you quote
> them. One thing to know: the data processing agreement covers business
> customers only, so a charity on Free or Pro hasn't got one.

**Reasoning.** First person because the workflow is Jasmin's and it is the most
useful sentence on the card. The catch is editorial, that cited output feels
verified when it is not, and it is the failure mode that will actually bite a
comms lead quoting a statistic in an appeal. The DPA point earns its clause
because A4 called it the sharpest contrast in the set and no field can show it.

### NotebookLM · Sheet row 40 · Amber · Research, Internal comms

> Upload the funder report, the strategy document, the sixty-page consultation
> response, and have a conversation with it instead of reading it twice. The
> Audio Overview turns any of that into something you can listen to on the way
> in, which is how I get through a board pack now. Free on Google for
> Nonprofits. It only sees what you put in a notebook, so the whole governance
> question is which documents you upload, and that's a decision you can actually
> make.

**Reasoning.** Straight from Jasmin's `my_stack` verdict, with the documents
made sector-specific. The governance sentence stays because on this row it is
reassurance rather than warning, which is worth saying when every other card
carries a caution.

### ElevenLabs · Sheet row 45 · Amber · Accessibility, Translation

> The accessibility work most small teams never get to: spoken versions of your
> annual report, your newsletter, your exhibition text. For heritage teams it
> also does audio guides without a studio. The Impact Program gives free access
> to nonprofits and cultural institutions on application and it names museums
> specifically, so ask. Synthetic narration still sounds synthetic to plenty of
> listeners, so it belongs where nobody was expecting a person, not on the chief
> executive's message. Your own scripts only: a recorded voice is personal data
> and it trains on what you give it unless you opt out.

**Reasoning.** One of two rows that speaks to cultural and heritage rather than
charity, and A4 recorded that the Impact Program names museums, so the draft
uses it. The main catch is editorial: knowing where a synthetic voice is
appropriate is a judgement no field can make. The consent line closes it because
a voice is the one input on this card that is personal data by definition.

### Descript · Sheet row 46 · Amber · Case studies & storytelling, Accessibility

> Edit the transcript and the video edits itself, which is what finally makes
> captioning and cutting possible for a team with no video editor. Interview
> footage, event recordings, a supporter telling their own story. The charity
> rate is twelve dollars an editor and eligibility reads 501(c)(3) or
> equivalent, so UK charities qualify and should ask. Two catches. Complex edits
> still want a real timeline, and editing someone's testimony by deleting words
> from a transcript is far too easy, so decide where your line is before you
> start.

**Reasoning.** The second catch is the one this sector needs and no other
directory would write: transcript editing makes it trivial to reshape what a
beneficiary said. That sits alongside section 7 of the policy template without
being a governance clause. The or equivalent wording is the fact that stops a UK
charity assuming a US-only scheme.

### Claude · Sheet row 57 · Amber · Case studies & storytelling, Appeals & fundraising, Internal comms

> My primary tool, and the argument for it is depth rather than breadth. Most
> people use it at surface level, a prompt at a time, and get generic output
> back. Set up a Project with your tone of voice, your last three appeals and
> your style guide in it, and it starts writing like your organisation rather
> than like an AI. That setup is the whole difference and it takes an afternoon.
> The nonprofit Team tier is eight dollars a seat, cheaper than the consumer
> plan and on better terms, which is a rare combination worth claiming.

**Reasoning.** Keeps Jasmin's own position from `my_stack`, that depth beats
breadth, and makes it actionable for this reader by naming what goes in the
Project. The catch is the setup cost, stated plainly. The pricing line is buying
advice rather than governance: the charity route is cheaper than the one most
people default to, which is rare enough to say.

### ChatGPT · Sheet row 58 · Amber · Research, Translation, Accessibility

> The one everyone already has, which is exactly why it's worth being deliberate
> about what you use it for. It's the best brainstormer here and the widest
> general knowledge, and voice mode is genuinely useful for thinking out loud on
> a commute. It's also agreeable, and it will tell you your appeal copy is
> strong when it isn't, so ask it to argue against you rather than to improve
> you. Keep donor records, financial reporting and anything about a named
> beneficiary out of it, and put that work on a tool that sits on your own
> tenancy.

**Reasoning.** The catch is sycophancy, which is the real risk for a comms lead
checking their own copy, and it is a use problem rather than a data one. The
closing line is Jasmin's preserved column L position almost intact, moved to
second person.

### Microsoft Copilot · Sheet row 60 · Amber · Internal comms, Accessibility

> You use this one for the reason you'd use a locked filing cabinet: it keeps
> the work inside your own tenancy. That makes it the right place for HR
> matters, financial reporting and first drafts of trustee papers, and it reads
> across SharePoint and Outlook at once, which nothing else here does. It isn't
> the best writer in this list and it doesn't need to be. Turn web search off
> before anyone uses it, because those queries leave the tenancy and Microsoft
> says so plainly.

**Reasoning.** The one row where governance leads, because governance is the use
case. Jasmin's column L opens on data governance rather than capability and that
is still the best sentence in the preserved material. The cross-SharePoint and
Outlook reach is the capability nobody else has, and saying plainly that it is
not the best writer is what stops the verdict reading as a sales line.

### Google Workspace AI · Sheet row 61 · Green · Internal comms

> Most useful in Sheets, where it does the jobs that eat an afternoon:
> standardising a donor export, cleaning inconsistent entries, reformatting a
> column someone typed by hand. The Gemini side panel in Docs pulls live data
> from Gmail or a connected sheet without leaving the document. It isn't the
> most capable model you can reach, it's the one already inside the tools you're
> in, and that's usually worth more. Free on Google for Nonprofits. Worth
> knowing the consumer Gemini app is a different proposition: it trains on your
> chats by default, this doesn't.

**Reasoning.** From Jasmin's `my_stack` verdict, with the Sheets jobs made
sector-specific because messy donor and volunteer exports are the daily reality
here. The Gemini contrast is one clause rather than the headline, which is the
correction from the first pass: it is useful, it is not what the card is for.

### Wispr Flow · Sheet row 63 · Amber · Accessibility, Internal comms

> It changed how I draft. Speaking is faster than typing and this works in
> whatever app you're already in, so it isn't a mode you switch into. Good for
> first drafts, for prompting, and for anyone on the team who finds typing slow
> or painful, which is a real accessibility gain nobody budgets for. Dictated
> drafts ramble, so it saves you time at the start and costs you some at the
> edit. It also hears everything: transcription is cloud-side, audio is stored
> by default, and there's a setting that reads what's on your screen. Switch it
> off before a supporter call.

**Reasoning.** First person because the claim is Jasmin's own and short. The
accessibility framing is the sector case and it belongs in a directory with an
Accessibility job. The rambling-drafts line is the honest cost. The last two
sentences are the one A4 finding nobody would ever discover alone, so they stay.

### Notion AI · Sheet row 65 · Amber · Internal comms, Case studies & storytelling

> Only worth it if Notion is already where your team keeps things, because the
> value is that it can see the rest of your workspace: the content calendar, the
> meeting notes, the register. Don't adopt Notion in order to get it. The
> stronger move is Notion as the structured place your other AI tools read and
> write, rather than Notion AI itself doing the thinking. The charity discount
> is half price on Business, which is the tier where the full AI lives, so free
> and Plus won't give you what you've read about.

**Reasoning.** Leads with the disqualifying condition, because most readers
should not adopt Notion to get Notion AI. The middle is Jasmin's own `my_stack`
position, that the integrations matter more than the AI, which is a more honest
recommendation than the tool's own marketing. The tier point stops a reader
assuming the discount applies to the plan they are on.

### DeepSeek · Sheet row 66 · Red · Case studies & storytelling

> Here because the axis has to show its working, not as a recommendation. The
> models are strong and very cheap, and the open weights mean a technically
> capable organisation could run them on its own infrastructure, which is a real
> argument for a charity watching every pound. That isn't the product most
> people would be using. The hosted service stores your data in the People's

**Reasoning.** The second row where governance leads, because it is why the row
is a public failure. Jasmin's column L made the honest budget and open-weights
case and cutting it would turn a judgement into a dismissal, so the draft keeps
it and separates the self-hosted question from the hosted product, which the
current verdict does not do at all.

## Pasting these

**The fifteen complete rows are not contiguous.** A block paste down column E
will overwrite rows that are not on this list. Eight paste operations:

| Paste | Sheet rows | Tools |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | E3 | HubSpot |
| 2 | E27:E29 | Canva, Adobe Creative Cloud, Adobe Express |
| 3 | E39:E40 | Perplexity, NotebookLM |
| 4 | E45:E46 | ElevenLabs, Descript |
| 5 | E57:E58 | Claude, ChatGPT |
| 6 | E60:E61 | Microsoft Copilot, Google Workspace AI |
| 7 | E63 | Wispr Flow |
| 8 | E65:E66 | Notion AI, DeepSeek |

**Every verdict contains commas and several contain colons.** Paste one cell at
a time, or paste each block from a single column, and do not route them through
anything that splits on a comma.

Column E is not one of the seven fields `isComplete` tests, so a bad paste here
will not hide a row. It will publish the wrong words, which is worse.
